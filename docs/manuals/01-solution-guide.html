1The idea in one page

A spaza shop is a small business that runs on trust, cash and a cell phone. TillKnowIT gives it the tools a large shop has, without asking it to change how it works.

Most till systems assume a fast connection, a fixed counter and a bookkeeper. A spaza shop has none of those. It has a phone, power that comes and goes, data that costs money, and a shelf of stock the owner keeps in their head. TillKnowIT is a point of sale built around that reality.

It starts at the till: scan, tap, take the money, done, even when the network is down. Around the till it adds what an owner actually needs to grow: stock that is counted, a credit book that is honest, daily numbers that arrive on the phone, suppliers who can be ordered from in a few taps, and, for school tuckshops, a wallet that lets a parent load money for a child and see what it was spent on.

Know it, done. The mark is a spaza awning over a tick. The name says the promise: know your shop, your stock and your money, and get it done.

Three promises the design keeps

Selling never waits for the network
A sale is written on the till first and sent when there is a connection. A shop that loses its line for a day loses no sales.
Money is never guessed
Every rand moves through a ledger that must balance to the cent, and every request that moves money is safe to repeat. Nothing is charged twice, and nothing disappears.
People keep control of their own information
Nothing about a customer, a child or a shop is shared without a recorded choice, and shared figures about an area never name anyone.

3What it does

The system was built in five phases, each one adding a layer on the last. The list below is what exists in the code today. Chapter 11 says which parts are connected to real outside services and which are not.

Phase 1: the spaza till

  • Owner sign-up, shop setup, staff with PINs, and tills registered with a one-time code.
  • A shared product catalogue, with each shop's own products and prices on top.
  • Selling by scan, search or tile, paid by cash, card, QR or on credit; slips only when a customer asks.
  • Stock in by scan, Excel or CSV, or a photo of an invoice; stock counts; expiry tracking (the rule that blocks expired goods is written, but the till's selling screen does not apply it yet, so for now expiry shows as flags on the stock list).
  • A credit book, cash-up at the end of the day, reports as CSV, Excel and PDF, and a compliance document wallet.
  • Airtime and electricity sales, a daily summary and loss alerts for the owner, and a field agent app to set shops up.

Phase 2: the supplier marketplace

  • Suppliers apply, are verified, upload a price list and set where and when they deliver.
  • Shops see ranked reorder options (true cost per unit including VAT, deals and delivery), order by app, WhatsApp or email, and receive against the order.
  • Payment can be held until delivery is confirmed, with refunds for short deliveries and a dispute process. Shops can buy together at a group price.

Phase 3: the school wallet

  • A double-entry ledger, school registration, learner import, cards and wristbands.
  • Parents top up, set a daily limit, block items, and see every purchase. Pre-orders by break time.
  • Paying at the till by card or QR, including offline within a signed allowance; daily payouts to the tuckshop and reconciliation to the cent.

Phase 4: every business

  • Business packs switch features on for a cafe, butchery, salon, clothing or hardware shop without changing the code.
  • Variants, add-ons, promotions and combos, refunds, split payments, open tickets, tables and a kitchen board, tips, weighed goods, quotes, lay-by, serial numbers and gift vouchers.
  • Appointments and commission, customers and loyalty points, printer, drawer and scale support behind a standard interface.

Phase 5: growth

  • Plans and billing, sponsored deals that are always labelled, and branches with combined reports and stock transfers.
  • Daily journals to Xero or Sage, pay-later through a named partner, area trend reports from shops that opt in, and a read-only partner API.

4How the parts fit together

One API, one worker, one database, and apps that talk to the API through a single typed client.

TillKnowIT is a modular monolith: a single deployable .NET 10 application made of modules that each own their own code and their own database schema. Modules never read one another's tables; they talk only through published contracts and events. That discipline is checked by automated architecture tests, so a module can later be moved into a service of its own without a rewrite.

6Selling without a connection

The till is the one app that must never stop. It is built so that a sale is safe on the device before anyone is told it succeeded.

  1. The cashier rings up the sale. The till works out prices, promotions and change using the same rules as the server, written in TypeScript so they run on the device.
  2. On payment, the sale is written to a queue on the device (the outbox) before the screen says it is saved.
  3. When there is a connection the till sends the queue in batches. If the network fails it tries again with longer pauses.
  4. The server checks each sale against the same rules, records it once, and answers. A sale sent twice is recognised by its id and never doubled.
  5. Anything the server refuses is counted and shown, so a problem is seen rather than hidden.

Some things need a connection by their nature: redeeming a gift voucher, online refunds, and any payment that must be checked against a live balance. The school wallet is the exception that proves the rule: each till is handed a signed allowance for each learner, so a card can be accepted offline without ever holding the real balance.

Offline sync of the shop's whole product list is not finished. The decision on the sync technology waits on a test against SQL Server (decision record 0001). Until then the till downloads its lists when it is online and uploads its sales in batches.

7Looking after money

Anything that moves money follows the same few rules, in every module.

Double entry
The wallet records every movement as legs that must add up to zero. The code refuses anything that does not balance, and a property test tries two thousand random cases of each kind of posting.
Balances are worked out, not stored
A balance is the sum of its entries. There is no number to be edited or to drift out of step.
Safe to repeat
Every request carries a reference. Sending it twice has the effect of sending it once. This is what makes retries after a dropped connection harmless.
Hold, then release
When a shop pays a supplier in the app, the money is held until delivery is confirmed (or released after 72 hours), short deliveries are refunded, and a dispute freezes the money until a platform admin resolves it.
Reconcile every day
A daily job compares the ledger with the payment partner's own total, to the cent. Any difference blocks payouts until a person writes down what happened.
Last, never first
For airtime and electricity the sale is saved before the partner is called. A reply that never arrives is recorded as unknown and is never retried, so no one is ever sold the same voucher twice.

8Privacy and security

Who can do what

People sign in with a cell number and a one-time code. They receive a signed session that lasts twelve hours, and every request that names an acting person must carry that person's token. Permissions come from membership of a shop, school or supplier, with a role. Tills prove themselves with a signed device token, issued once from a setup code the owner approves, and that can be reset.

What is protected

  • Documents and photos are stored encrypted (AES-256-GCM) and downloaded only by people allowed to see them.
  • PINs are stored as salted hashes, never as the PIN, and a till locks after five wrong tries.
  • Cards are stored as a hash of the NFC identifier. A blocked card stays blocked.
  • Vouchers are shown once; only a hash is kept.
  • Partner callbacks are signed and checked for replay, and accepted once only.

What is shared, and only by choice

  • A parent sees a child only after entering the link code and agreeing to the terms; the version of the terms agreed to is recorded.
  • Supplier demand totals and area trend reports show counts and bands, never names, and never from fewer than three shops (demand) or ten shops (trends). The ten-shop rule is enforced by the code and by a database constraint.
  • Sharing to a pay-later partner needs consent for that named partner, can be withdrawn, and every share is logged where the owner can see it.
  • Erasure removes names, grades, photos and guardian links; the ledger stays, because it holds no names.

The legal and data-ethics review for market insights and pay-later sharing (POPIA and competition law), the penetration test and a load test are still to be done before any real shop is asked to opt in. They are on the hand-over list in the technical guide.

9One product, many kinds of business

A spaza shop needs a till and a credit book. A cafe needs tables and a kitchen. A salon needs appointments and commission. A butcher sells by weight. A hardware shop quotes and keeps serial numbers.

TillKnowIT does not build a different product for each. A business pack is a named list of features and settings, stored as data. The owner picks a business type; the features it includes appear, and the others stay out of sight. Switching changes only what is visible, so no product, sale or customer is touched and switching back restores everything. A platform admin can create a new pack with no code change, and the architecture tests make sure no pack name ever appears in the selling code.

12What comes next

  1. Run it for real. Run the API and worker against a real SQL Server and confirm the migrations, the seed and the outbox behave.
  2. Connect the partners. SMS, payments, the stored-value partner, WhatsApp, Xero and Sage, and a finance partner, each in its sandbox first.
  3. Review before release. Legal and data-ethics review, a penetration test, a load test at ten thousand shops, and first-language review of every translation.
  4. Choose the hardware. Write the real printer, drawer, scale and NFC adapters once the devices are chosen and tested.
  5. Pilot with twenty to thirty shops and let what they actually do decide what to build next.
